Lecture 28

Reconstruction and the boundary of quantum groups

What this lecture does

The group, its coefficient algebra and its tensor representations encode the same global information in different forms. Differentiation connects these descriptions to the enveloping algebra. It loses information, however, unless the allowed group representations are retained. We make that connection explicit and identify the structures that a later course on quantum groups will deform.

28.3 What the dual contains

For any unital algebra AAA, its finite dual A∘A^\circA∘ consists of the functionals that vanish on a two-sided ideal of finite codimension. Equivalently they are matrix coefficients of finite-dimensional AAA-modules. Here is the elementary equivalence.

A finite-dimensional module gives a homomorphism A→End⁡(V)A\to\End(V)A→End(V) with finite-codimensional kernel; every coefficient vanishes on that kernel. Conversely, if φ\varphiφ vanishes on such an ideal III, use the finite-dimensional regular module A/IA/IA/I, vector 1+I1+I1+I and functional φˉ\bar\varphiφˉ​. A finite-dimensional representation of kC\algk_\CkC​ need not respect the periods of KKK or the restrictions imposed by a central quotient.

28.4 Global forms and tensor reconstruction

The groups SU⁡(2)\SU(2)SU(2) and SO⁡(3)\SO(3)SO(3) have isomorphic real Lie algebras, hence the same complex enveloping algebra after this identification. Their coefficient algebras retain different representations. On VmV_mVm​ it acts by (−1)m(-1)^m(−1)m. The algebraic Peter–Weyl decomposition therefore identifies the right side with precisely the coefficient blocks for even mmm. Odd blocks have been excluded, not annihilated by differentiation. Both coefficient algebras inject into the same finite dual, with different images.

For SU⁡(2)\SU(2)SU(2) the image fills the finite dual. More generally, let KKK be compact, connected and simply connected. A finite-dimensional module of UUU restricts to a Lie algebra map from k\algkk to gl(V)\gl(V)gl(V). By the integration theorem of Lecture 4, this map is the differential of a representation of KKK. So every element of U∘U^\circU∘ is a coefficient of a representation of KKK. With Proposition 28.3, the pairing identifies R(K)\mathcal R(K)R(K) with U∘U^\circU∘. Thus U∘U^\circU∘ gives KCK_\CKC​, whose points are the characters of R(K)\mathcal R(K)R(K) by Lecture 14, and the involution of the first section selects KKK. The involution is needed: U(sl(2,C))U(\sllie(2,\C))U(sl(2,C)) is also the enveloping algebra of the complexification of su(1,1)\suu(1,1)su(1,1). Tori and central quotients are where the coefficient algebra adds information. The circle example shows a torus, and (28.3) shows a quotient.

Let KCK_\CKC​ be the complexification of KKK introduced in Lecture 14. It is a complex Lie group, and its identity component is reductive. The reconstruction theorem is cited from Bröcker–tom Dieck, Sections III.7–8. It identifies K≅Aut⁡⊗u(ω),KC≅Aut⁡⊗(ω).K\cong\operatorname{Aut}^{u}_{\otimes}(\omega),\qquad K_\C\cong\operatorname{Aut}_{\otimes}(\omega).K≅Aut⊗u​(ω),KC​≅Aut⊗​(ω). The unitary condition distinguishes the compact target from its complexification.

A circle quotient and its representation lattice

There is a smaller example of global information in a coefficient subalgebra. Let μN⊂U⁡(1)\mu_N\subset\UU(1)μN​⊂U(1) be the NNNth roots of unity. The quotient is again a circle, but keep the quotient map and the identification of Lie algebras fixed. Under the same infinitesimal pairing, its evaluation points are iNZiN\mathbb ZiNZ, instead of iZi\mathbb ZiZ. The real Lie algebras of these two circles are isomorphic. The quotient map, however, has changed which periods a representation must respect. Rescaling an abstract Lie algebra isomorphism does not erase the specified quotient map.

The subalgebra remains closed under the Hopf operations. For its generator w=zNw=z^Nw=zN, Δw=w⊗w,εw=1,Sw=w−1,w∗=w−1.\Delta w=w\otimes w,\qquad \varepsilon w=1,\qquad Sw=w^{-1},\qquad w^*=w^{-1}.Δw=w⊗w,εw=1,Sw=w−1,w∗=w−1. The same calculation with even highest weights gives the SU⁡(2)\SU(2)SU(2) to SO⁡(3)\SO(3)SO(3) distinction. In both cases, keeping a Lie algebra while changing the allowed coefficient subalgebra changes the global representation problem.

28.5 What a quantum-group course changes

The structures above give a concrete starting point for deformation. The coefficient algebra encodes the multiplication of group elements in its coproduct. The enveloping algebra encodes the infinitesimal bracket in its associative product. The pairing between them relates these two operations. A deformation can change these operations while retaining the Hopf identities.

Classical object Product Coproduct
R(K)\mathcal R(K)R(K) commutative usually not cocommutative
U(kC)U(\algk_\C)U(kC​) usually not commutative cocommutative

A noncommutative coefficient algebra is no longer an algebra of scalar-valued functions on an ordinary group. A noncocommutative coproduct changes how tensor products of representations carry an action. The order of tensor factors can then matter. Associativity of the tensor product of representations still follows from coassociativity.

Here is one such deformation, as an algebraic calculation only.

A braiding is additional structure, not an automatic property of a Hopf algebra. In suitable quasitriangular examples on the enveloping side, an RRR-matrix supplies an intertwiner between the two tensor orders. The future course will define these notions and prove their compatibility identities. They are not needed for any theorem here.

The ∗*∗-structure also remains substantive. An arbitrary Hopf ∗*∗-algebra is not automatically a compact quantum group; positivity and analytic completion require further hypotheses.

The useful preparation is therefore the classical bookkeeping developed in this course: distinguish an algebraic coefficient space from a completion, track coproduct order, keep the real or unitary structure, and retain the global representation lattice. These are the data a deformation must replace or preserve. The theory of deformation parameters, Uq(g)U_q(\g)Uq​(g) and roots of unity begins in the later course.

Looking back

We asked what a group determines that its Lie algebra does not. One answer runs through the course. A connected Lie group is a quotient of its simply connected covering group by a discrete central subgroup. In an irreducible representation of the cover that is finite-dimensional or unitary, a central element acts by a scalar. So a global form chooses which central characters are allowed. We used this for SU⁡(2)\SU(2)SU(2) and SO⁡(3)\SO(3)SO(3) in Lecture 4 and for lattices in Lecture 7 and Lecture 8. It separates integer and half-integer spin in Lecture 13, in the Lorentz modules of Lecture 24, in the massive states of Lecture 25, and on the sphere of Lecture 27. The coefficient algebras of this lecture record the same choice.

Part I treated compact groups. For a compact connected group, the irreducible representations are labelled by dominant characters. For every compact Lie group, the invariant integral leads to a decomposition of L2(K)L^2(K)L2(K) as a Hilbert sum of finite blocks.

Part II removed compactness. Generators, the modular function and induction joined the invariant integral as the working tools. We then described the unitary representations of the Heisenberg group, of SL⁡(2,R)\SL(2,\R)SL(2,R) and of the Poincaré group. Each description has a cited theorem at its centre. For the Heisenberg group, we proved that the Schrödinger representation is irreducible, and the Stone–von Neumann theorem says that it is the only one for each nonzero central character. For SL⁡(2,R)\SL(2,\R)SL(2,R) we constructed the principal and discrete series and derived the positivity bounds, and the full list is cited from Bargmann and Harish-Chandra. For the Poincaré group, Mackey’s theorem reduces the problem to little groups. We computed the massive and finite-helicity sectors, and we only named the others.

Orbits organise much of this. In Lecture 19 a coadjoint orbit of the Heisenberg group is a plane or a point, and it gives one representation. In Lecture 27 the nonzero coadjoint orbits of SU⁡(2)\SU(2)SU(2) are spheres. For m≥1m\ge1m≥1, the sphere of area 2πℏm2\pi\hbar m2πℏm gives VmV_mVm​, on holomorphic sections of a line bundle over the sphere. The point orbit gives V0V_0V0​. In Lecture 17 and Lecture 25 the orbits lie instead in the dual of the translations.

We did not decompose L2(G)L^2(G)L2(G) for noncompact GGG, apart from the statement cited in Lecture 23, and we did not treat general semisimple groups. These are the next subjects in the representation theory of Lie groups. A second direction deforms the Hopf structures of this lecture, and a later course on quantum groups begins there.

Summary

Differentiation pairs representative functions with the enveloping algebra. For connected KKK, the pairing embeds them in the finite dual. There the global group selects a specific subalgebra, which is the whole finite dual when KKK is simply connected. Tensor reconstruction recovers KKK when unitary structure is kept and KCK_\CKC​ when it is forgotten. The coefficient and enveloping Hopf structures provide the classical starting point for quantum groups.
