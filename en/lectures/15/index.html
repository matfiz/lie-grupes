What this lecture does

This lecture fixes the continuity hypothesis, defines the derivative on smooth vectors, and uses Stone’s theorem to identify the skew-adjoint generator of each one-parameter subgroup, together with its domain. Translations on L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R) are the model throughout.

15.1 From matrices to Hilbert spaces

Let Π ⁣:G→GL⁡(V)\Pi\colon G\to\GL(V)Π:G→GL(V) be a continuous representation on a finite-dimensional complex space VVV. Continuous homomorphisms of Lie groups are smooth (Lecture 3). For compact GGG, the Peter–Weyl theorem (Lecture 10) decomposes each unitary representation into finite-dimensional subrepresentations. That theory rests on averaging. The Haar integral of a compact group has finite total mass, so Lecture 5 can average an inner product, and Lecture 10 can average an operator. A noncompact Lie group also has an invariant integral, but its total mass is infinite (Lecture 16). So it has no normalised finite Haar integral, and these averaging arguments are no longer available in general.

The theory changes in the following ways. First, a finite-dimensional representation need not preserve any inner product. For example, for m≥1m\ge1m≥1 and t≠0t\ne0t=0, the matrix diag⁡(et,e−t)∈SL⁡(2,R)\diag(e^t,e^{-t})\in\SL(2,\R)diag(et,e−t)∈SL(2,R) acts on the module VmV_mVm​ with the eigenvalue emte^{mt}emt, and a unitary operator has eigenvalues of modulus one. Lecture 23 proves more: every finite-dimensional unitary representation of SL⁡(2,R)\SL(2,\R)SL(2,R) is trivial. So a nontrivial unitary representation of this group is infinite-dimensional.

This is not a property of every noncompact group. The group R\RR has the one-dimensional unitary characters x↦eicxx\mapsto e^{icx}x↦eicx. It shows another change. Its translation representation on L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R) is unitary, and it has no nonzero finite-dimensional invariant subspace (Proposition 15.4 below). So a unitary representation need not contain any finite-dimensional piece.

Finally, on an infinite-dimensional space the derivative of a representation may be defined only on a proper subspace. We begin with this point, on translations. Lecture 16 and Lecture 17 then build Haar measure and induction without compactness. We apply these tools to the Heisenberg group, to SL⁡(2,R)\SL(2,\R)SL(2,R), and to the Lorentz and Poincaré groups. Lecture 26 and Lecture 27 return to compact groups, and Lecture 28 returns to Lecture 14.

15.8 Summary

Unitary representations are required to be strongly continuous, not norm continuous. The translation representation on L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R) has no nonzero finite-dimensional invariant subspace. Smooth vectors form a dense invariant subspace H∞\mathcal H^\inftyH∞. On it, dΠd\PidΠ is a representation of g\gg by skew-symmetric operators. Stone’s theorem attaches a skew-adjoint generator to each strongly continuous unitary representation of R\RR. The closure of dΠ(X)d\Pi(X)dΠ(X) on H∞\mathcal H^\inftyH∞ is this generator. For translations, −d/dx-d/dx−d/dx on S(R)\mathcal S(\R)S(R) is skew-symmetric but not skew-adjoint. Its closure has the strictly larger domain D(A)D(A)D(A), and D(A)D(A)D(A) is still not all of L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R).
