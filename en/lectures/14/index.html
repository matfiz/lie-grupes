Lecture 14

Representative functions and tensor reconstruction

What this lecture does

This lecture asks what information about a compact Lie group KKK is contained in all its representations. We collect all matrix coefficients into one algebra R(K)\Rcoeff(K)R(K). The group law becomes three maps on this algebra. We then state how KKK is recovered, and what is recovered when the unitary structure is forgotten.

A space can be replaced by the algebra of functions on it. A group has more: its product appears as a map on these functions. This description still makes sense for algebras that are not commutative, and Lecture 28 returns to them. Nothing here depends on that.

14.5 The representation category

The Category Theory course studied fusion categories, such as SU⁡(2)k\SU(2)_kSU(2)k​, with finitely many simple objects. The Clebsch–Gordan rule has the same form. We show that an infinite compact Lie group falls outside this class, and that finiteness is the only axiom that fails.

We use the notions of a rigid and of a symmetric monoidal category from the prerequisite course. Rigidity requires duals, with evaluation and coevaluation maps that satisfy the zigzag identities. A symmetry is a natural isomorphism τV,W ⁣:V⊗W→W⊗V\tau_{V,W}\colon V\otimes W\to W\otimes VτV,W​:V⊗W→W⊗V with τW,VτV,W=id⁡\tau_{W,V}\tau_{V,W}=\idτW,V​τV,W​=id, compatible with associativity.

Definition 14.13. A fusion category is a C\CC-linear semisimple rigid monoidal category with finite-dimensional morphism spaces, simple unit object, and finitely many isomorphism classes of simple objects.

The category Rep⁡fd(K)\Rep_{\mathrm{fd}}(K)Repfd​(K) is semisimple (Lecture 5) with simple unit C\CC and finite-dimensional morphism spaces. It is rigid, with the dual representation and evV(λ⊗v)=λ(v),coevV(1)=∑iei⊗ei.\mathrm{ev}_V(\lambda\otimes v)=\lambda(v),\qquad \mathrm{coev}_V(1)=\sum_ie_i\otimes e^i.evV​(λ⊗v)=λ(v),coevV​(1)=i∑​ei​⊗ei. Summary

Part I in review

Part I asked what a group determines that its Lie algebra does not. A finite-dimensional representation of a Lie algebra integrates to the simply connected group, and it descends to a quotient exactly when the central kernel acts trivially (Lecture 4). This step does not use compactness. For a compact connected group the test reads from the lattice of a maximal torus, and the irreducible representations are labelled by dominant characters (Lecture 7 and Lecture 8). So SU⁡(2)\SU(2)SU(2) has every VmV_mVm​, and SO⁡(3)\SO(3)SO(3) has only those with mmm even. The lift of a full spatial turn acts on VmV_mVm​ by (−1)m(-1)^m(−1)m. This sign fixes every ray, so Lecture 13 passes to projective representations.

Compactness gave the rest of Part I. The Haar integral has finite total mass. Averaging gives invariant inner products, complete reducibility and Schur orthogonality (Lecture 5). Averaging a rank-one projection gives a nonzero compact operator, and the Peter–Weyl theorem follows (Lecture 10). So every irreducible unitary representation is finite-dimensional, and L2(K)L^2(K)L2(K) is a Hilbert sum of blocks of matrix coefficients. Lecture 11 and Lecture 12 read this as Fourier analysis and as a count of multiplicities on K/HK/HK/H. This lecture recovers KKK from its coefficient algebra, by a cited theorem.

A noncompact group has no normalised finite Haar integral, so these averaging arguments are no longer available in general. Part II starts from that point.
