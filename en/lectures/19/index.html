Lecture 19

Coadjoint orbits and the nilpotent orbit method

What this lecture does

A representation acts on vectors. The coadjoint action of a Lie group instead acts on linear functionals on its Lie algebra. Each orbit of this action has a natural symplectic form. For connected simply connected nilpotent groups, these orbits label all irreducible strongly continuous unitary representations up to unitary equivalence. We prove the geometric construction and work through the Heisenberg example. The general classification is an explicit cited theorem.

19.4 Polarisations and the orbit theorem

A Lie algebra is nilpotent if its lower central series terminates: g1=g,gr+1=[g,gr],gs=0 for some s.\g_1=\g,\qquad \g_{r+1}=[\g,\g_r],\qquad \g_s=0\text{ for some }s.g1​=g,gr+1​=[g,gr​],gs​=0 for some s. For the Heisenberg algebra, g2=RZ\g_2=\R Zg2​=RZ and g3=0\g_3=0g3​=0. Nilpotence is stronger than solvability.

We import a global fact: for a connected simply connected nilpotent group, exp⁡:g→G\exp:\g\to Gexp:g→G is a diffeomorphism, and BCH is a finite polynomial. Every Lie subalgebra m\mathfrak mm gives a closed subgroup M=exp⁡mM=\exp\mathfrak mM=expm. Indeed, finite BCH keeps products in m\mathfrak mm, and the exponential diffeomorphism takes the closed vector subspace m\mathfrak mm to a closed subset. See Knapp, Lie Groups Beyond an Introduction, Section I.16.

Definition 19.3. A real polarisation for ℓ∈g∗\ell\in\g^*ℓ∈g∗ is a Lie subalgebra m⊂g\mathfrak m\subset\gm⊂g such that ℓ([m,m])=0,dim⁡m=dim⁡g+dim⁡gℓ2.\ell([\mathfrak m,\mathfrak m])=0,\qquad \dim\mathfrak m=\frac{\dim\g+\dim\g_\ell}{2}.ℓ([m,m])=0,dimm=2dimg+dimgℓ​​.

This dimension says that the image of m\mathfrak mm in g/gℓ\g/\g_\ellg/gℓ​ is as large as the image of an isotropic subspace can be. Theorem 19.4 (Nilpotent orbit theorem; cited). Let GGG be connected, simply connected and nilpotent. Every ℓ∈g∗\ell\in\g^*ℓ∈g∗ has a real polarisation. The unitary induced representation Πℓ=Ind⁡exp⁡mGχℓ\Pi_\ell=\operatorname{Ind}_{\exp\mathfrak m}^{G}\chi_\ellΠℓ​=IndexpmG​χℓ​ is irreducible. Its equivalence class does not depend on the polarisation. This is Kirillov’s theorem, imported from Lectures on the Orbit Method, Chapter 3. Induction means unitary induction with the quotient measure convention of Lecture 17. The statement concerns complex Hilbert representations, not just finite-dimensional modules.

The global assumptions matter. A central quotient can restrict which representations descend. A solvable group need not be nilpotent. Apart from the trivial group, no compact group and no semisimple group satisfies this theorem’s hypotheses. We will meet related orbit constructions in Lecture 27, with a different integrality condition and a different representation model.

What the Heisenberg orbit list explains

The orbit calculation separates two phenomena. For λ≠0\lambda\ne0λ=0, changing ppp or qqq stays within one plane. It cannot change the equivalence class of the representation. For λ=0\lambda=0λ=0, each point is an orbit of its own, so ppp and qqq both survive as representation labels. The discontinuous change in orbit dimension matches the change from infinite-dimensional representations to characters.

One can check the zero-charge conclusion without the general orbit theorem. In an irreducible unitary representation, every central group element acts by a scalar, by the unitary Schur lemma used in Lecture 18. If that scalar character is trivial, the representation factors through H/Z(H)≅R2H/Z(H)\cong\R^2H/Z(H)≅R2. An irreducible unitary representation of this abelian group is one-dimensional. Its continuous characters are ei(pa+qb)e^{i(pa+qb)}ei(pa+qb), obtained by applying the one-parameter character classification to each coordinate.

For nonzero central character, the Stone–von Neumann theorem gives uniqueness directly. The orbit theorem recovers that result and explains its geometric label. It also shows what to try for a larger nilpotent group: calculate ad⁡∗\ad^*ad∗, find orbit invariants, determine the stabiliser dimension, choose a real polarisation for ℓ\ellℓ, and induce its character. The last exercise carries out the geometric steps in one four-dimensional example.

Self-checks

1. Why must the stabiliser algebra contain the centre?

Answer. If XXX is central, then [X,Y]=0[X,Y]=0[X,Y]=0 for every YYY. The stabiliser formula (19.1) puts XXX in gℓ\g_\ellgℓ​ for every ℓ\ellℓ.

2. Does the formula ωλ=λ dp∧dq\omega_\lambda=\lambda\,dp\wedge dqωλ​=λdp∧dq have the right normalisation?

Answer. No. Its value on (−λ∂q,λ∂p)(-\lambda\partial_q,\lambda\partial_p)(−λ∂q​,λ∂p​) would be λ3\lambda^3λ3. The required value is λ\lambdaλ, so the coefficient is 1/λ1/\lambda1/λ.

3. Can one apply the orbit theorem directly to a Heisenberg group with circle centre?

Answer. No. That group is not simply connected. Apply the theorem to its simply connected cover. Then use the Descent theorem of Lecture 4. Only representations trivial on the covering kernel descend.

Summary

The coadjoint action turns g∗\g^*g∗ into a union of even-dimensional symplectic orbits. For connected simply connected nilpotent groups, a polarisation converts an orbit into an irreducible induced representation. For the Heisenberg group, planes give Schrödinger representations and points give characters. The cited classification is exact in this setting. Its hypotheses are part of the result.
